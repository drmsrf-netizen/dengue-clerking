Dengue fluid management · CPG summary

Summary of fluid management from the CPG Management of Dengue in Adults (4th edition). Tap a heading to expand it. Use the Fluid calculator above for this patient's rates.
Key principles
  • Fluid therapy is the main life-saving treatment. Aim for enough intravascular volume through the 24–48 h leak, then reduce early to avoid overload when reabsorption begins.
  • Crystalloid is the fluid of choice. Adjust frequently based on clinical response, not a fixed regimen.
  • Judge response on clinical condition, vital signs, CCTVR and urine output together. Never bolus on a single parameter.
  • Blood and blood products count towards the cumulative fluid total.

Common pitfalls

  • Boluses in the febrile phase; prolonged fixed regimens in stable patients; IV fluids continued into recovery.
  • Infrequent monitoring and late adjustment of rates.
  • Boluses for a rising HCT, tachycardia or cool peripheries alone, without full assessment.
  • Colloids (e.g. albumin) used for maintenance.
  • Excess fluid in heart failure or kidney disease.

Febrile phase
  • Keep a cannula in situ. Encourage oral fluids.

Critical phase: recognition and fluid quota

Indicators of critical phase

  • Rapid drop in temperature (some still have low-grade fever early in the leak).
  • HCT rise ≥20% from baseline.
  • Platelets <100 ×10⁹/L (earliest and most useful indicator).
  • Plasma leakage on POCUS: pericholecystic fluid, thickened GB wall, ascites, pleural effusion.

Leak usually peaks around 24 h and resolves within 48 h. Patients presenting in shock may already be ~24 h into the leak; those with uncorrected dehydration may be early in it.

Fluid quota (critical phase without shock)

  • Maintenance + 5% deficit (5% = 50 ml/kg), oral and IV combined, as the maximum over 48 h. Calculate at the start of the critical phase.
  • Not a target: many patients need less.
  • Does not apply once the patient is in shock, or in DKA/HHS.
  • Example, 60 kg: 2,400 ml (1 day maintenance) + 3,000 ml = 5,400 ml over 48 h.

Urine output

Algorithm 1 · warning signs, no shock (Group B2)
  1. Start IV crystalloid 3–5 ml/kg/hr for 1 hour.
  2. Improving: reduce to 2–3 ml/kg/hr for 1–2 hours, then 1–2 ml/kg/hr or maintenance, then stop IV when able to tolerate orally (oral intake 1–2 ml/kg/hr makes IV unnecessary).
  3. Not improving and not in shock: take FBC/VBG/lactate; increase to 5–7 ml/kg/hr while awaiting results and titrate.
  4. Shock develops: go to Algorithm 2 or 3.

Total fluid must stay within the 48 h quota. FBC every 4–12 hours in the critical phase.

Algorithm 2 · compensated shock (Group C)

Definition: SBP maintained with signs of reduced perfusion: a combination of tachycardia, cold peripheries, CRT >3 s, PP 20–30 mmHg, low pulse volume.

  1. Send FBC, RP, LFT, RBS, PT/APTT, CK, VBG/lactate/HCO₃, GXM. Give crystalloid 10 ml/kg/hr for 1 hour.
  2. Improving: 5–7 ml/kg/hr for 1–2 h → 3–5 ml/kg/hr for 2–4 h → 2–3 ml/kg/hr for 2–4 h → reduce further. HCT 4–6 hourly.
  3. Not improving: repeat HCT and run a second 10 ml/kg/hr crystalloid bolus while waiting.
    • HCT dropped ≥20%: consider significant occult/overt bleed → Algorithm 4.
    • HCT rose ≥20% or unchanged: continue the second bolus. Still not improving → repeat HCT and give colloid 10–20 ml/kg/hr for 1 hour, then switch back to crystalloid once improving.

After stabilising: HCT rise ≥20% → consider increasing regimen; HCT fall → consider transfusion. Shock may recur. Consider stopping IV fluid at 48 h of plasma leakage.

Algorithm 3 · decompensated shock (Group C)

Definition: hypotension with signs of reduced perfusion: tachycardia/bradycardia, cold clammy, CRT >3 s, PP <20 mmHg, thready pulse.

  1. Send HCT/FBC, RP, LFT, RBS, PT/APTT, CK, lactate/HCO₃, GXM. Give 20 ml/kg crystalloid over 15–30 minutes.
  2. Improving: 10 ml/kg/hr for 1 h → 5–7 ml/kg/hr for 2–4 h → 3–5 ml/kg/hr for 2–4 h → 2–3 ml/kg/hr for 2–4 h → reduce further. HCT 2–4 hourly or more often.
  3. Not improving: repeat HCT and run a second bolus of 10–20 ml/kg colloid or crystalloid while waiting.
    • HCT rose ≥20% or unchanged: continue 10–20 ml/kg over 30–60 minutes.
    • HCT dropped ≥20%: suspect significant bleed; transfuse packed red cells and/or blood components.
    • Still not improving → Algorithm 4.

Manage shock in HDU/ICU, but do not delay resuscitation while waiting for the bed. Consider stopping IV fluid at 48 h of plasma leakage.

Algorithm 4 · refractory / persistent shock

Use POCUS and haemodynamic assessment to identify the type of shock (more than one may coexist):

HypovolaemicBleeding and leaking: find the source (e.g. OGDS), look for leak (USG, CXR), check coagulopathy, transfuse packed red cells and components.
DistributiveSeptic shock: antibiotics + vasopressors. Cytokine storm: vasopressors ± steroids. Acute liver failure: supportive care + vasopressors.
CardiogenicMyocardial dysfunction: inotropes (noradrenaline, dobutamine, adrenaline, milrinone). Arrhythmia: anti-arrhythmics.
ObstructiveCardiac tamponade: pericardiocentesis after expert consultation.
  • Further fluid guided by fluid responsiveness (preferably passive leg raise with direct CO/SV measurement; PPV/SVV or EEOT if ventilated) and fluid tolerance (breathlessness, rising O₂ need, pulmonary oedema, raised JVP, B-lines/VExUS).
  • IVC measurement is an adjunct only, never the sole guide.
  • Persistent shock with high HCT → consider colloid. With dropped HCT → suspect occult bleeding and transfuse promptly.
  • Noradrenaline is the preferred vasopressor for shock persisting despite adequate fluid.
Choice of fluid and blood products
  • Crystalloid first line: balanced (Hartmann's, Sterofundin) or 0.9% saline. No proven outcome difference; balanced solutions cause less hyperchloraemia.
  • Colloid: albumin 5% is the colloid of choice, for persistent shock only. Avoid gelatin. Never use colloid for maintenance.
  • Blood: packed red cells 5–10 ml/kg for significant bleeding; repeat if further loss or no appropriate HCT rise. Do not wait for a marked HCT fall, as earlier haemoconcentration masks it.
  • Fibrinogen <100 mg/dL → cryoprecipitate. Fibrinogen ≥100 with PT/APTT >1.5× normal → consider FFP. Correct coagulopathy only with clinically significant bleeding.
Signs of effective resuscitation

Clinical

  • Better general well-being / mental state
  • Warm peripheries
  • CRT ≤3 s
  • Stable BP
  • Improving pulse pressure
  • Reducing tachycardia
  • Improving urine output
  • Less tachypnoea

Laboratory

  • Appropriate decrease in HCT
  • Improving metabolic acidosis and lactate

Interpret all parameters together, not in isolation.

Recovery phase
  • Signs: improving appetite and well-being, convalescent rash, diuresis, rising platelets, stable HCT, normalising organ function.
  • Reduce or stop IV fluids promptly to avoid overload. POCUS can show plasma resorption before clinical improvement.
  • Restart withheld antihypertensives gradually once haemodynamically stable.
Special populations
Heart failureEarly admission. Reduce fluid volumes. Early cardiology input. Consider loop diuretics if overloaded and haemodynamically stable.
CKD 3b+ / ESKDEarly admission. Reduce fluid volumes. Early nephrology input for KRT planning if oliguric. Interpret HCT against the patient's own baseline.
Liver cirrhosisIndividualised fluids with close monitoring. Risk of GI bleed and severe shock. IV vitamin K if PT/INR prolonged.
Diabetes / DKA / HHSDKA/HHS: follow the DKA/HHS fluid regimen (may exceed quota). Osmotic diuresis can give falsely reassuring urine output. Stop oral hypoglycaemics; use insulin.
HypertensionA "normal" BP may be relative hypotension: compare with baseline. Consider withholding antihypertensives in the critical phase.
ElderlyIndividualised, cautious fluids: high risk of overload, pulmonary oedema and heart failure.
PregnancySame approach as non-pregnant, given cautiously with monitoring for pulmonary oedema. Multidisciplinary care.
ObesityUse ABW-based dosing with frequent monitoring, especially in shock.
